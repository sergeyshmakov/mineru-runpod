The template's default pool list is `ADA_24,AMPERE_24,AMPERE_48`: RTX 4090 first, then RTX A5000/3090, then RTX A6000. On 2.x, MinerU 4.1 runs the `MinerU2.5-Pro-2605-1.2B` VLM on vLLM for the `standard` and `advanced` tiers and sizes vLLM to about 6 GB on any card, so 24 GB leaves room to spare. In the 1.x measurements, the 4090 was both faster per page and cheaper per page than the lower-hourly A5000/3090 (`AMPERE_24`). This page covers the cases where a different trade-off makes sense.

Figures labelled 1.x on this page were measured on MinerU 3.4; their 2.x equivalents are still being measured.

## TL;DR

| You are… | Use GPU pool | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Parsing small-to-medium docs (≤100 pages) one at a time | `ADA_24` (default) or `AMPERE_24` | On 1.x the 4090 was ~4× faster per warm page than the A5000, and supply is better. A5000 has lower $/hr if you can tolerate slower parses. |
| Parsing large books or huge documents (100–10,000+ pages) | `ADA_24` (default), **batched** | Length isn't what fills the card: vLLM's memory is fixed when the engine starts, and MinerU works through a PDF in windows of 64 pages. Slice the doc into `page_range` jobs client-side. On 1.x we ran 5,039 pages this way on a 24 GB A5000. See [Large documents](#large-documents-batch-dont-size-up). |
| Running several jobs inside one worker | `ADA_24` with [`MINERU_VIRTUAL_VRAM_SIZE`](#mineru_virtual_vram_size) raised; `AMPERE_48` once that runs out | MinerU gives vLLM about 6 GB on any card, so a 48 GB card adds nothing by itself — raising vLLM's share does. Measure first. |

If you're unsure, **start with the default pools and watch for OOM in the worker logs**. Changing pools is a one-line change to `deploy.py --gpu-ids` or the `gpuIds` field in your endpoint config.

## How much VRAM MinerU actually uses

MinerU 4 sizes vLLM itself when the engine starts. On cards with more than 8 GB it sets vLLM's `gpu_memory_utilization` to `max(6 / VRAM in GB, 0.1)`, rounded to two decimals — about **6 GB for the VLM on any card**, weights, activations and KV cache included:

| Card | `gpu_memory_utilization` | vLLM's share |
|---|---|---|
| 24 GB (`ADA_24`, `AMPERE_24`) | 0.25 | ≈6 GB |
| 48 GB (`AMPERE_48`) | 0.12 | ≈6 GB |
| 80 GB (`AMPERE_80`) | 0.1 (the floor) | ≈8 GB |

MinerU 3.x, which 1.x ran, used a flat 0.5 instead: about 12 GB of a 24 GB card, of which vLLM's KV cache took 9.5 GiB.

The rest of the card is left to MinerU's small models — layout, OCR, formulas and tables, which run on the same GPU under PyTorch — and to PyTorch's own caches. `flash` and `basic` never start vLLM at all.

{/* MEASURE: 2.x VRAM breakdown on a 24 GB card at the default tier — vLLM weights, KV cache, CUDA graphs, small models, peak total — for a one-page and a 100+-page PDF. Replaces the 1.x table that stood here (~13 GiB peak, 9.5 GiB KV cache at gpu_memory_utilization=0.5). */}

Implications:

- **24 GB is the default and has headroom.** Whether `standard` now fits a 16 GB card — about 6 GB for vLLM plus the small models — is plausible on paper but not measured, so no 16 GB pool is in the defaults.
- **Length doesn't grow the budget.** vLLM's share is fixed at engine start, and MinerU renders and analyses a PDF `MINERU_PROCESSING_WINDOW_SIZE` pages (64 by default) at a time. On 1.x, a **5,039-page** PDF parsed on a 24 GB A5000, sliced into page-range batches client-side ([walkthrough](/blog/2026-06-03-clause-aligned-batching-large-pdf-mineru/)); a 2.x re-run is pending. So **don't size up the GPU for a big document — batch it** (see [Large documents](#large-documents-batch-dont-size-up)).
- **In-worker concurrency has less room than on 1.x.** Jobs running in one worker share vLLM's KV cache, and on 2.x that cache is what's left of ≈6 GB after the weights, not 9.5 GiB. A bigger card doesn't change that by itself — `MINERU_VIRTUAL_VRAM_SIZE` does.

{/* MEASURE: peak VRAM of standard and advanced on a 16 GB card; peak VRAM of one long (1,000+ page) standard parse on 24 GB. */}

### `MINERU_VIRTUAL_VRAM_SIZE`

MinerU sizes itself as if the card had this many GB, so vLLM gets `6 / MINERU_VIRTUAL_VRAM_SIZE` of the **real** card. Set it as an endpoint environment variable, in whole GB; empty means the detected VRAM.

| Setting | On a 24 GB card | On a 48 GB card |
|---|---|---|
| unset | 0.25 → ≈6 GB | 0.12 → ≈6 GB |
| `16` | 0.38 → ≈9 GB | 0.38 → ≈18 GB |
| `12` | 0.5 → ≈12 GB, what 1.x used | 0.5 → ≈24 GB |

A larger share is a larger KV cache, which is what lets several concurrent jobs batch through one engine — so on 2.x this, not the pool, is the dial for in-worker concurrency. It also takes memory from the small models, so raise it in steps and watch VRAM. Values of 6 or less ask vLLM for the whole card or more, which fails.

{/* MEASURE: throughput and peak VRAM on ADA_24 with MINERU_VIRTUAL_VRAM_SIZE unset vs 12, at MINERU_MAX_CONCURRENCY 1, 2 and 3. */}

### What each tier puts on the GPU

| Tier | On the GPU |
|---|---|
| `flash` | Nothing for text-layer PDFs and native documents; MinerU's small models for the OCR pass on scans and images |
| `basic` | MinerU's small models (layout, OCR, formulas, tables) |
| `standard`, `advanced` | The small models plus vLLM's ≈6 GB |

Once vLLM is loaded it keeps its share for the life of the worker, and the boot warmup loads the tier in `MINERU_WARMUP_TIER` — `standard` by default. An endpoint that only serves `flash` or `basic` should set `MINERU_WARMUP_TIER` to match.

## Why the MinerU paper used 48 GB

The published benchmarks ran on dual RTX 4090 (effectively 48 GB combined) to **maximize throughput** for the leaderboard numbers — not because correctness requires it. If you're running a single document at a time in a serverless worker, you do not need 48 GB.

## Workload-to-GPU map

Concurrency comes from two independent levers — `workers_max` (more workers) and `MINERU_MAX_CONCURRENCY` (more jobs per worker); see [Concurrency](/guides/concurrency/) for the full model. Horizontal `workers_max` on 24 GB workers is the default throughput dial.

### Single worker, one doc at a time

This is the default RunPod serverless shape: each incoming job spins a worker, the worker parses the document, the worker scales to zero after `idle_timeout`. No concurrency inside the worker.

- **GPU pool**: `ADA_24` (default) or `AMPERE_24` (cheaper hourly, slower)
- **VRAM**: ≈6 GB for vLLM plus the small models; the peak is not yet measured on 2.x
- **Tier**: `standard` (MinerU's default)

### Single worker, batching multiple docs

If you set `MINERU_MAX_CONCURRENCY` > 1 (RunPod calls this the `concurrencyModifier`) so one worker handles several requests at once, their pages share one vLLM engine and its KV cache, while MinerU's small-model stages take turns on the GPU. Worth it only when single requests don't saturate the GPU (small or I/O-heavy docs) — otherwise add a worker instead. See [Concurrency → in-worker](/guides/concurrency/#niche-in-worker-concurrency).

- **GPU pool**: `ADA_24` with `MINERU_VIRTUAL_VRAM_SIZE` raised; `AMPERE_48` once 24 GB runs out
- **VRAM**: not yet measured on 2.x
- **Trade-off**: higher utilization per worker, but how much it pays on 2.x hasn't been measured. Watch `nvidia-smi` in worker logs after deploy.

### Multiple workers, one doc each (horizontal scaling)

This is what RunPod does when you set `workers_max > 1` and several requests arrive at once. **Each worker is an isolated container with its own model copy** — VRAM is per-worker, not shared.

- **GPU pool**: `ADA_24` per worker
- **VRAM**: per worker, as in the single-worker case
- **Scaling note**: you pay per worker-second, so cost scales linearly. The default `workers_max: 3` (set in `deploy.py` / the dashboard, not `hub.json`) is a sensible cap for spiky workloads.

### Multiple workers, in-worker concurrency on each

The maximum-throughput shape: `workers_max > 1` AND `MINERU_MAX_CONCURRENCY > 1`. Each worker runs several jobs through its shared engine while RunPod scales horizontally.

- **GPU pool**: `ADA_24` with `MINERU_VIRTUAL_VRAM_SIZE` raised, or `AMPERE_48` with it raised further
- **VRAM**: not yet measured on 2.x
- **When to use**: high-volume ingest of small or I/O-heavy docs where you've already validated that in-worker concurrency beats simply adding 24 GB workers.

## Document-size adjustments

| Doc size | Pages | Recommended pool | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tiny | 1–3 | `ADA_24` | Default 24 GB pool. 16 GB pools are not in the defaults; 2.x may fit them, unmeasured. |
| Small | 3–30 | `ADA_24` | The "average document" case the defaults are tuned for. |
| Medium | 30–100 | `ADA_24` | Same default; vLLM's share doesn't change with page count. |
| Large | 100–1,000 | `ADA_24`, batched | Batch into page-range jobs (below) for resumability and to stay under the 20 MB response cap — not because 24 GB runs out. |
| Huge | 1,000–10,000 | `ADA_24`, batched | Same: don't size up. On 1.x we parsed a 5,039-page PDF on a 24 GB A5000, batched client-side. |

### Large documents: batch, don't size up

A bigger document is **not** a reason for a bigger GPU. vLLM's memory is fixed when the engine starts and MinerU works through a PDF window by window, so a 1,000- or 10,000-page PDF needs the same card as a 10-page one; on 1.x, a single sequential parse peaked at ~13 GiB at any page count. What breaks on big documents isn't VRAM — it's everything *around* the parse:

- **All-or-nothing failure** — one job that dies at page 4,800 (OOM, transient eviction, timeout) costs the whole run.
- **The [20 MB response cap](/blog/2026-05-20-runpod-20mb-response-cap-r2-bridge/)** — a few hundred pages of output already blows past it.
- **No resumability** — one job has no checkpoint; a failure means starting over.

The fix is **client-side page-range batching**, not a 48 GB card. The worker slices server-side: pass a `page_range` such as `"1-200"`, then `"201-400"` ([API reference](/reference/api/#job-input)), and each job parses that range out of the same uploaded file — you never pre-split the PDF. Each batch's `page_idx` values are already the pages' positions in the whole document, so don't add the batch's start page to them as 1.x required. Keep `MINERU_MAX_CONCURRENCY=1` and scale throughput with `workers_max` (more GPU workers, not a bigger GPU). See [Concurrency](/guides/concurrency/) for how `workers_max` and `MINERU_MAX_CONCURRENCY` interact.

<Callout type="info" title="The worker does not auto-batch">
It parses one page range per job; splitting a large document into batches is something *you* do on the client. The full walkthrough — clause-aligned boundaries, R2 transport for huge output, and the throughput math — is in [Clause-aligned batching for large PDFs](/blog/2026-06-03-clause-aligned-batching-large-pdf-mineru/), where a **5,039-page** specification parsed on a 24 GB A5000 for ~$1.15 on 1.x. The walkthrough slices with 1.x's 0-based `start_page`/`end_page`; on 2.x the same slice is a 1-based `page_range`.
</Callout>

## Picking a tier

MinerU 4.1 replaces 1.x's backends with four quality tiers. Select one per job with `tier`; the same worker serves every tier. Native documents (DOCX, XLSX, HTML, …) always run at `flash` — see [Input formats](/guides/input-formats/#tiers-and-formats).

| Tier | What runs | MinerU's description | Loads the VLM |
|---|---|---|---|
| `flash` | Nothing for text-layer PDFs and native documents; a fast OCR pass for scans and images | Lowest quality, fastest: previews and indexing | No |
| `basic` | MinerU's small models: layout, OCR, formulas, tables | Basic quality; lower-resource parsing | No |
| `standard` *(default)* | The small models plus the `MinerU2.5-Pro-2605-1.2B` VLM, with local layout driving the VLM | Standard high quality; MinerU's default | Yes |
| `advanced` | The VLM does its own layout and content, with image analysis on | Better on difficult documents; slowest | Yes |

Coming from 1.x, MinerU's own advice is not to infer new quality defaults from old backend names. As starting points for a comparison on your own documents:

| 1.x backend | Compare on 2.x |
|---|---|
| `vlm-auto-engine` (the 1.x default) | `standard` (the new default, cheaper) and `advanced` (the closest to it) |
| `hybrid-auto-engine` | `standard`; `advanced` for 1.x's `effort: "high"` |
| `pipeline` | `basic` — but see [OCR languages](/guides/input-formats/#ocr-languages) for non-Latin scans |
| `vlm-http-client` | `advanced` with `server_url` |
| `hybrid-http-client` | `standard` with `server_url`; `advanced` for 1.x's `effort: "high"` |

The full field mapping is in [Upgrading to 2.x](/getting-started/upgrade-to-2/).

### flash and basic: no VLM

Neither tier starts vLLM, so neither pays for its memory or its load time. `flash` is MinerU's tier for previews and indexing, at the lowest quality; `basic` runs the small models only, MinerU's choice for lower-resource parsing. Both read scanned pages with MinerU's single OCR recognizer, which covers Chinese, English, Japanese and Latin-script languages only — scans in other scripts belong on `standard` or `advanced` ([OCR languages](/guides/input-formats/#ocr-languages)).

```json
{
  "input": {
    "file_url": "https://example.com/scan.png",
    "tier": "basic"
  }
}
```

An endpoint that only serves these tiers should set `MINERU_WARMUP_TIER` to match: the default warmup runs `standard`, which loads the VLM at boot.

### standard and advanced: the VLM

Both load the VLM through vLLM, with the ≈6 GB share described [above](#how-much-vram-mineru-actually-uses). `standard` lets the small models find the layout and has the VLM read it; `advanced` has the VLM do both, with image analysis on, and is the slowest tier — MinerU's choice for difficult documents when a longer wait is acceptable.

On 1.x, the same VLM ran at roughly 1–6 s per warm page on a 4090 and 1–10 s on an A5000, depending on content density, against MinerU's published ~0.5 s/page (2.12 fps) on an A100. The 2.x figures per tier are pending.

### External VLM server (`server_url`)

On `standard` and `advanced`, a job can send its VLM requests to an external OpenAI-compatible server instead of the local engine by passing `server_url`. The small models still run in the worker; only the VLM moves. Useful when you want to:

- Keep a fleet of small workers cheap and stateless
- Run one always-on vLLM server amortising the model load across all of them
- Run vLLM on hardware the serverless tier doesn't offer (multi-GPU, NVLink, etc.)

```json
{
  "input": {
    "file_url": "...",
    "tier": "standard",
    "server_url": "https://your-vllm-host.example.com/v1"
  }
}
```

`server_url` is rejected on `flash` and `basic`, which have no VLM to replace. A worker that only ever uses it should set `MINERU_WARMUP_TIER=basic`, or the boot warmup loads the local VLM anyway. Endpoints upgraded from 1.x must delete `MINERU_VL_MODEL_NAME`, which MinerU 4 refuses alongside a per-job `server_url` — see [Troubleshooting](/guides/troubleshooting/#mineru_vl_model_name-conflicts-with-modelvlmmodel). For endpoints open to callers you don't control, see [URL inputs](/reference/api/#url-inputs).

### MinerU's tiers and runtimes (upstream)

Mirrored from MinerU's [official PyPI page](https://pypi.org/project/mineru/) so you don't have to context-switch. These are MinerU's project-wide requirements for local parsing; this image runs the last row.

| Tier | Model engines | Model download | Min RAM | Accelerator |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `basic` | ONNX | ~0.8 GB | 2 GB | None (CPU works) |
| `basic` | PyTorch | ~0.8 GB | 8 GB | GPU/MPS recommended |
| `standard` / `advanced` | ONNX + llama.cpp | ~2 GB | 8 GB | CPU works, Vulkan recommended |
| `standard` / `advanced` | PyTorch + llama.cpp | ~2 GB | 16 GB | GPU/MPS required, 8 GB+ VRAM |
| `standard` / `advanced` | PyTorch + vLLM/LMDeploy/MLX | ~3 GB | 16 GB | GPU/MPS required, 8 GB+ VRAM |

`flash` isn't in MinerU's table: it runs no VLM, and needs no models at all for text-layer PDFs and native documents. The image bakes the PyTorch small models (`opendatalab/MinerU-4_models_torch`, 0.89 GB) and the VLM (`opendatalab/MinerU2.5-Pro-2605-1.2B`, 2.3 GB), and serves the VLM with vLLM 0.21. MinerU's 8 GB VRAM figure is its floor for the VLM tiers; this template's defaults start at 24 GB.

## How to configure GPU pool

Three places, in order of how you'd reach for them:

### From `deploy.py`

```powershell
python deploy.py --template-id $env:MINERU_TEMPLATE_ID --gpu-ids AMPERE_48
```

Accepts a comma-separated list — RunPod picks the first available pool from the list at scale-up time. The repo's default already prefers 4090 then falls back to A5000 then to A6000:

```powershell
python deploy.py --template-id ... --gpu-ids "ADA_24,AMPERE_24,AMPERE_48"
```

### From the RunPod dashboard

Endpoint detail page → **GPU configuration** → tick the pools you want. Same comma-separated semantics: RunPod tries the cheapest available pool that's allow-listed.

### From `.runpod/hub.json`

For published Hub templates, the `gpuIds` field declares the default pool list users will see when they deploy your template:

```json
{
  "config": {
    "gpuIds": "ADA_24,AMPERE_24,AMPERE_48",
    ...
  }
}
```

This repo's default exposes 24 GB and 48 GB tiers so users can opt up without re-publishing. **You can change the pool list per-endpoint from the RunPod dashboard at any time** — the `hub.json` value is just the suggested starting set for new deploys.

## Supported GPU pools — full reference

The Hub default is intentionally conservative. The full list of RunPod pools that work (or are still being verified) with this template:

| Pool ID | What it contains | Compute | Status on 2.x |
|---|---|---|---|
| `ADA_24` *(default)* | RTX 4090, 24 GB | 8.9 | ✅ Default. On 1.x: ~1–6 s/page VLM warm depending on content density, the best speed-and-cost trade-off for sequential parsing. 2.x figures pending. |
| `AMPERE_24` *(default)* | RTX 3090 / RTX A5000, 24 GB | 8.6 | ✅ Default. On 1.x: ~1–10 s/page VLM warm. Lower $/hr than ADA_24 but slower per page; usually worse $/page in practice. |
| `AMPERE_48` *(default)* | RTX A6000, 48 GB | 8.6 | ✅ In default list; same generation as A5000, double the VRAM. MinerU still gives vLLM ≈6 GB here unless `MINERU_VIRTUAL_VRAM_SIZE` raises it. |
| `AMPERE_80` | NVIDIA A100 80 GB | 8.0 | ✅ The GPU MinerU benchmarks against (2.12 fps / ~0.5 s/page upstream). Supported by vLLM; untested by us. MinerU's 0.1 floor gives vLLM ≈8 GB. |
| `ADA_80_PRO` | RTX 6000 Ada variant | 8.9 | ✅ Should work. Untested by us. |
| `ADA_48_PRO` | RTX 6000 Ada *or* RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell — RunPod groups them together | 8.9 *or* **12.0** | ⚠️ Not in the default list: RunPod can schedule a Blackwell card here, and Blackwell is being verified for 2.x. See [the Blackwell note](#a-note-on-ada_48_pro-and-blackwell). |
| `ADA_32_PRO` | RTX 5090, 32 GB | 12.0 | ⏳ Blackwell, being verified for 2.x. Not in the default list. |
| `BLACKWELL_96` | RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell, 96 GB | 12.0 | ⏳ Being verified for 2.x. Not in the default list. |
| `BLACKWELL_180` | B200, 180 GB | 10.0 | ⏳ Being verified for 2.x. Not in the default list. |

Other pools (`HOPPER_*`, lower-VRAM Ampere SKUs, etc.) may exist in your RunPod account but I haven't verified the exact pool-ID strings. RunPod's pool IDs have been historically inconsistent — `ADA_48` vs `ADA_48_PRO` was a footgun we hit on this project. **Before adding a non-default pool to your endpoint, check RunPod's [GPU types reference](https://docs.runpod.io/references/gpu-types)** to confirm the exact ID string.

To opt into a non-default pool, edit your endpoint's **GPU configuration** in the RunPod dashboard and add the pool ID to the comma-separated list. RunPod picks the first available pool from the list at scale-up time, so listing cheaper options first keeps cost lowest while making expensive options available if cheaper ones run out.

**Why we don't put A100/H100 in the Hub default**: the `hub.json` `gpuIds` list is what new deployers see on the deploy form. Including `AMPERE_80` by default would make it look like A100 is the expected GPU for this template — it's not. Per-page cost on A100 is often *higher* than on ADA_24 because the hourly rate jump outpaces the speed-up except for sustained throughput. ADA_24 is the right default; opt into bigger only when you've measured a workload that needs it.

## A note on `ADA_48_PRO` and Blackwell

The `ADA_48_PRO` pool was originally the RTX 6000 Ada Generation (compute capability 8.9). RunPod has been adding the newer RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell cards (compute capability 12.0) to the same pool, and the names are nearly identical — NVIDIA labels both as "RTX 6000" family cards.

On 1.x that mattered. The image's vLLM 0.11.2 shipped attention kernels for Ampere (8.0–8.6), Ada (8.9) and Hopper (9.0) but no Blackwell (12.0) path, so the VLM crashed during model init on a Blackwell card:

```
CUDA error (...flash-attention/hopper/flash_fwd_launch_template.h:188): invalid argument
```

2.x runs vLLM 0.21, the release MinerU 4 validates its own image on, and upstream vLLM supports Blackwell — so the blocker behind that crash is gone on paper. The template doesn't claim Blackwell support until a live smoke test on these pools passes, and the default `gpuIds` stay `ADA_24,AMPERE_24,AMPERE_48`, all unambiguously pre-Blackwell. If you try a Blackwell pool (`ADA_48_PRO`, `ADA_32_PRO`, `BLACKWELL_96`, `BLACKWELL_180`), do it on a separate endpoint first, and check that a `standard` job succeeds with `debug.gpu.compute_capability` reading `12.0` (`10.0` on a B200).

## Cost implications

GPU pricing on RunPod varies by pool, availability, and whether you're on spot vs on-demand. Indicative serverless Flex rates we've seen (subject to change — confirm at [RunPod's pricing page](https://www.runpod.io/pricing)). The per-page times are 1.x measurements — MinerU 3.4's VLM backend, warm, on dense content; 2.x figures per tier are pending.

| Pool | $/hr | Per-page wall time (1.x, warm VLM, dense content) | Per-page cost (1.x) |
|---|---|---|---|
| `ADA_24` (RTX 4090, default) | ~$1.10 | ~1–6 s | **lowest** |
| `AMPERE_24` (A5000 / 3090) | ~$0.69 | ~1–10 s | ~1.5–2× higher than ADA_24 despite cheaper hourly — speed gap dominates |
| `AMPERE_48` (A6000) | ~$0.79 | similar to A5000 | only worth it when 24 GB OOMs |
| `AMPERE_80` (A100 80 GB) | ~$1.89 | ~0.5 s upstream | sustained throughput only; otherwise per-page cost is *higher* than ADA_24 |

On 1.x, ADA_24 won on $/page despite the higher hourly: the 4090's compute speed-up (~2–4× faster per page than A5000) outpaced its ~1.6× hourly premium. The math only flips when a workload sits on a single warm worker for very long stretches and the per-page advantage saturates.

## When to revisit this

Watch your worker logs after deploy. The signals that mean you should change something:

- `CUDA out of memory` errors → lower `MINERU_MAX_CONCURRENCY` or `MINERU_PROCESSING_WINDOW_SIZE` first, then move to the next VRAM tier — see [CUDA out of memory](/guides/troubleshooting/#cuda-out-of-memory)
- vLLM warnings about KV cache space or preempted requests → reduce concurrency, or raise vLLM's share with `MINERU_VIRTUAL_VRAM_SIZE`
- Per-page wall time spiking → check `nvidia-smi`; if at 100% memory but low utilisation, you're memory-bound

Conversely, a 48 GB pool buys little on 2.x unless you've raised vLLM's share with `MINERU_VIRTUAL_VRAM_SIZE`. If VRAM use on one stays low, drop to 24 GB.
